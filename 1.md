Valued resources are defined as things that have value and are subject to allocation/exchange. Valuation is what allows a resource to be recognized, after which it can be compared and differentiated from others, establishing its relative worth. This enables resources to be compared, allocated, exchanged, and managed within their environment.

A valued resource that can interact with an environment and result in dynamical behavior within it is called Economy.

Economy: the study of how value interact.

We can see this in how early humans traded and shared beliefs about what was considered valuable. This is similar on how people developed what is considered moral or good/bad, but applied to resources. These resources can include services, such as skills, or physical objects. For example, someone might ask another person to hunt for them and pay them with berries, or people might trade fish for chickens.

Is merely an expansion of the idea that scarity is fundamental to what an economy is. But lets look at an example on why economic might develop without scarity:

Consider a hypothetical society in which a particular resource is available in practically unlimited quantities. If everyone can obtain as much of that resource as they want without competing for it, then scarcity is no longer the primary problem surrounding that resource. However, people (considering that people possess different skills and personalities, which creates a diversity of capabilities, but is this truly scarcity, or simply divergence?) could still assign different values to other things, exchange resources or services, specialize in different activities, and develop systems for determining what should be produced, exchanged, or maintained.

Scarcity can explain why resources must sometimes be allocated between competing uses, but it does not necessarily explain why resources are valued, exchanged, or interacted with in the first place.

The distinction can therefore be made between the existence of an economy and the problems faced within an economy. Scarcity may be fundamental to understanding certain economic problems, but it does not necessarily have to be fundamental to the existence of economic activity itself.

From this perspective, a more fundamental question may be: how do valued resources interact with one another and with the environment in which they exist?

Under this interpretation, scarcity becomes one possible property of an economic environment rather than the defining property of an economy. An economy may operate under conditions of scarcity, abundance, or varying degrees of both, while the underlying interaction between valued resources remains.

Think about it this way: In 2015 the labor force in the United States contained over 158 million workers, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. The total land area was 3,794,101 square miles. While these are certainly large numbers, they are not infinite. Because these resources are limited, so are the numbers of goods and services we produce with them. Combine this with the fact that human wants seem to be virtually infinite, and you can see why scarcity is a problem. 

Hence, scarcity can be understood as one of the problems that arises from how people interact with a condition of valued resources (in this case limited valued resources). The economy itself, however, is concerned with understanding how valued resources behave and interact within an environment.
